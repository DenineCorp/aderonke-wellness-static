"When is my mother picking me up?" Her mother died eleven years ago. Do you say so — again — and watch her grieve a loss she won't remember learning about an hour from now? Or do you say something else?

This is one of the most common, and most emotionally loaded, decisions dementia caregivers face. The Alzheimer's Association describes the practice of not correcting a false belief — sometimes called "therapeutic fibbing" — as an effective but genuinely controversial strategy, and it's worth understanding both why it's recommended and where its limits are.

## Why correcting the facts can do more harm than good

As dementia progresses, a person's grip on current reality weakens, but their emotions stay fully intact. Correcting them — "Mom died eleven years ago, remember?" — rarely produces understanding. It usually produces either fresh grief (if the loss registers at all) or confusion and distress at being told they're wrong about something they're certain of. And because short-term memory is often what's most impaired, the correction frequently needs to happen again minutes later, repeating the pain each time for no lasting benefit.

The goal of dementia communication isn't to keep the person accurately informed — at later stages, that's often no longer possible. The goal is to reduce their distress and preserve their dignity.

## Try these first, before an outright fib

Non-correction doesn't have to mean lying. There's a spectrum, and it's worth working through the gentler options before reaching for a fib:

1. **Validation.** Acknowledge the feeling behind the question without confirming or denying the fact. "You miss your mom" responds to the emotion in "when is my mother picking me up" without requiring a factual answer at all.
2. **Redirection.** Shift attention to something engaging. "Let's go see what's happening in the kitchen" can gently move past a question without confronting it head-on.
3. **A simplified, non-confrontational answer.** The Alzheimer's Association's own example: if someone insists there's nothing wrong with them and resists a doctor's appointment, don't recite their diagnosis — say "it's just a regular check-up." It's not a lie about reality, but it sidesteps an argument the person can't productively have.

## When an outright fib is the kinder option

Sometimes none of the above fits, and a direct fib — "She's running a little late, let's have a snack while we wait" — is what actually keeps someone calm. Experts generally consider this defensible when:

- The person can no longer process or retain a correction anyway
- The honest answer would cause acute, repeated distress with no benefit
- The fib reduces real suffering, rather than just making the conversation easier for you

One common exception: early in a grief-related question, many dementia care experts suggest it's reasonable to answer honestly once or twice, even knowing it won't be retained — some things are worth saying straight, even briefly. It's later repetitions of the same painful answer, with no benefit to the person, where fibbing becomes the kinder path.

## The risk worth knowing about

The main downside isn't an abstract ethical one — it's practical. If a person with dementia catches an inconsistency (a fib contradicted by something else they can still track), it can damage trust in a way that makes future caregiving harder. This is part of why experts encourage trying validation and redirection first: they carry none of that risk.

It's also worth being honest with yourself about the "why." A fib that spares someone real distress is different from a fib that's really about avoiding a hard conversation you'd rather not have. The first is caregiving. The second is worth noticing in yourself.

## The bottom line

There's no single rule that covers every situation, because every person's dementia, history, and triggers are different. What's consistent across the guidance: start with validation and redirection, reserve direct fibs for when they genuinely reduce suffering rather than just convenience, and judge success by the person's calm, not by factual accuracy.
